      How this was built, and its limits

      • Five research passes covered gas and pipelines, private equity, traders and hedge funds, sovereign funds and state oil companies, and oil majors with Koch and Berkshire Hathaway. Each connection had to rest on a page the researcher opened, preferring filings, company releases, government records and major news outlets.
      • A separate fact-check pass, which had not seen the research, re-opened every cited page. Connections it could not confirm were removed; ones with a wrong detail were rewritten to say only what the sources support. Each card shows its check status.
      • Only connections that form part of a complete chain from the left column to the right column appear on this page.
      • Sources from advocacy groups (for example Greenpeace, the Private Equity Stakeholder Project, InfluenceMap) are labelled and can be hidden with the switch above the map.
      • Where an organization has responded to or disputed a claim, its response is shown under the claim.
      • Ownership changes. Several stakes are dated to when they were announced; the claim says so where current ownership was not confirmed.
      • Columns are a simplification: an oil major can also run a trading desk. Each organization sits in the column that best fits its role in these connections.
      • A line on this map records a relationship that sources document. It does not show intent, coordination or illegal conduct, and no such claim is made.

          How the money and votes were matched, and the limits

          • Contributions come from each PAC's itemized FEC disbursement records for the 2021–22, 2023–24 and 2025–26 election cycles, read from fec.gov. Counted: payments the filings tie to a member's FEC candidate ID, which means their principal campaign committee (whatever its name; Rep. Madeleine Dean's, for example, is called MAD 4 PA PAC) and, in some cases, a joint fundraising committee for that member. Not counted: leadership PACs, party committees and state candidates. Checked against FEC's list of leadership PACs on Nov 25, 2026; $38,000 that FEC linked to Rep. Ryan Zinke but that went to his leadership PAC was removed. The 2026 cycle runs to each PAC's latest filing and is incomplete.
          • A $0 cycle means the PAC gave nothing to current members' campaigns that cycle. For example, BP's PAC gave in 2021–22 only to the national party committees of both parties, in equal amounts, and to state legislative candidates.
          • Corporate PACs are funded by voluntary donations from employees, not company treasury money. The figures are net of refunds.
          • Recipients were matched to current members by FEC candidate ID, office, state and name. A review removed five matches that were a different person with the same surname (for example, former Sen. Bob Menendez and Rep. Rob Menendez) and added eight members whose FEC name was misspelled or listed under a different state.
          • Votes come from official Senate and House roll-call records. The two 2015 Senate amendments are scored together: a senator counts on the anti-climate side only if they voted against both, because some senators opposed the weaker wording while backing the stronger one.
          • Kinder Morgan, Blackstone and KKR have no corporate federal PAC in FEC records, and Equinor and Moody's report none, so they don't appear here. Their executives or employees may give individually; that isn't included.
          • Receiving money and voting a certain way happen together in these records. This page does not show that one caused the other.
